grip-lab: A Parametric Kit of
3D-Printed Grip and Finger Trainers for Bouldering

Paul Wang

Palo Alto, California

November 7, 2026

Abstract

We describe a ten-part kit of 3D-printed bouldering trainers. The kit covers the four grip types that limit most boulderers (crimps, pinches, slopers and pockets), plus antagonist work, wrist work and a load-cell gauge for measurement. One rule governs the whole design: steel carries every tension load, and printed plastic only sees compression or short, deep bending. FDM prints are weakest between layers and creep under sustained load, so no part ever relies on a printed body alone to hold you. Every part is generated from code, in build123d. Its dimensions come from typed TOML parameters, and automated checks gate each export. The kit is intended for sale both as printable files and as finished printed units. Because file buyers choose their own printer and filament, each part is sized for a published 150 kg one-handed rating with a safety factor of 3, using the interlayer strength of the weakest permitted material (PETG). This draft reports the design rules, the parts list, the software pipeline, six tolerance coupons that calibrate fits before any large print, and the three priority-one designs: a modular edge block and an adjustable pinch block, whose plastic sections and steel sizes are set by the structural checks, and a load-cell gauge housing. None of the trainers has yet been printed or load-tested.

Keywords: bouldering, grip training, fused deposition modelling, parametric CAD, build123d, interlayer strength, tolerance calibration

Introduction

Finger strength is the main limiter in bouldering, and the usual tools for training it, such as a hangboard, a lifting block or a portable edge, are simple shapes. That makes them good candidates for 3D printing, because one parametric model can produce any edge depth, radius or width. The risk is structural. A fused-deposition print is a stack of welded layers. Its tensile strength across those layers (the Z direction) is often half its in-plane strength or less, and it is lower again after long-term creep. A printed hold that you hang from loads exactly that weak direction.

The kit avoids the problem by design rather than by margin. Every load path from hand to weight runs through steel: a bolt, a rod, an eye nut or a Dyneema loop. The printed body is clamped between steel washers so that it is loaded only in compression, or in short in-plane bending at the lip of an edge. Most parts are lift blocks: you grip them and lift a loading pin off the floor, so a failure drops the weight a few centimetres instead of dropping you.

Design principles

Every part must satisfy the eight rules below. Those that can be checked automatically are enforced as tests that fail the build.

  1. Steel carries tension. Any load between hand and weight runs through steel or Dyneema. Printed bodies are clamped in compression.
  2. Orient for in-plane bending. Edges, lips and pinch jaws print so that the bending load lies in the layer plane. Edge cassettes print on their side, with the edge profile drawn in XY.
  3. No load in Z. If interlayer tension would exceed about 20 % of a feature's peak load, the feature is redesigned or split into parts that print flat.
  4. Material. PETG is the minimum. PA6-CF or PA12-CF is preferred for anything you pull on. PLA is banned for load parts because it is brittle and creeps.
  5. Print rules. 6 perimeters, 4 mm solid top and bottom, 40 % gyroid infill, 0.2 mm layers and a 0.4 mm nozzle. No supports on loaded faces. Overhangs are 45° or less, or bridged under 10 mm.
  6. Fillets where load turns a corner. Every loaded inside corner has a radius of at least 3 mm.
  7. Proof test before use. Hold 1.5× the maximum load for 30 s. Retire the part at any whitening, creaking, crack or permanent set.
  8. Retire on schedule. Re-inspect monthly. Replace load parts after 12 months or after one drop onto concrete.

Priority-one parts

Modular Edge Block.
A central spine is clamped between two M10 fender washers [4] by a threaded rod, with a nut on top and an eye nut [3] below. Edge cassettes slide on sideways along a horizontal 60° dovetail on both faces, and a ball-lock pin stops them sliding. Each cassette is reversible, so six cassettes give edges of 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 18, 20, 22 and 25 mm, plus two spare profiles. The edge faces down, has a parametric front radius (6 mm by default) and an optional 3° incut, and carries a modelled 0.25 mm stipple that adds friction without abrasive grit. Section 7 gives the finished design.
Smart Load-Cell Gauge.
A 200 kg S-beam load cell with M12 threads sits directly in the tension line between two M12 eye nuts. The printed housing carries no load. An electronics sled (ESP32-S3, HX711, 1000 mAh LiPo, USB-C) streams force over Bluetooth Low Energy to a web app, which shows peak force, rate of force development and repeater curves. Section 9 gives the housing.
Adjustable Pinch Block.
Two jaws ride on two ∅16 mm steel rods through a centre hub, which an M10 rod and eye nut clamp vertically. Width adjusts from 50 to 120 mm in 5 mm steps with clip-on spacer sleeves, and the jaw faces come flat, 5° tapered or rounded. Section 8 gives the finished design.

Design load and materials

The published rating is a working load of m = 150 kg, one-handed. With a safety factor S = 3, every structural check is sized against the design force

Fd = S m g = 3 × 150 kg × 9.81 m s−2 ≈ 4.41 kN.

Pockets carry the highest risk of pulley injury, so the pocket block is rated at a reduced load, kp m with kp = 0.4, which is 60 kg. Wherever a load path crosses layers, the allowable stress is the material's interlayer (Z) tensile strength divided by the safety factor:

σallow = σZ / S,   e.g. PETG: 25 MPa / 3 ≈ 8.3 MPa.

File buyers choose their own filament, so the design material is the weakest load-rated option (PETG), not the one we print with in house. Table 2 lists the working values. They are conservative estimates from vendor data sheets, and coupon testing will replace them.1

Implementation

The kit is a Python 3.12 package built on build123d [1], which uses the OpenCascade B-rep kernel [2]. Each part is code. Every dimension comes from a TOML file, either the shared defaults, the fastener table or the part's own parameters, and is validated by a Pydantic model with units in its field names (depth_mm, angle_deg). Clearances live in exactly one place, defaults.toml [clearance], so that tuning them to a printer is a one-line change.

Parts are assembled from a shared feature library: edge profiles, 60° dovetails, bolt bores, counterbores and washer seats, horizontal teardrop bores whose 45° roofs need no supports, detent hole rows, heat-set insert pockets, a stipple texture, engraved labels, and a fillet helper that refuses any loaded radius under 3 mm. The modelling frame is the print frame: the build plate is the XY plane at Z = 0, so every exported part already carries its print orientation.

Before export, each object must be a valid solid, produce a watertight mesh whose volume is within 2 % of the B-rep volume, sit on the build plate, and fit a 220 mm cube. That cube is the smallest bed we support for file buyers. A part that fails any check is not exported. Passing parts are written as STEP, as 3MF with one named object per piece, and as a hidden-line SVG drawing. Continuous integration repeats the lint (ruff), strict type check (mypy), unit and property-based tests (pytest, Hypothesis) and full build on every commit. The Hypothesis tests sweep parameter ranges to find combinations that break the geometry.

Until the coupons are printed, the kit uses the generic clearances in Table 4. They are chosen so that roughly 80 % of current, well-maintained printers can assemble the parts without sanding or force. The values deliberately err loose. A fit that is too tight fails to assemble, or cracks a part that is forced. Slight play is harmless here, because steel carries the load and the printed bodies are clamped. FDM holes typically print 0.1–0.3 mm undersize, and the values already allow for this. Each coupon ladder is centred on its generic value, so a printed coupon can tune the fit in either direction.

Modular edge block

A lift block hangs below the hand, so the fingers must push it up. The crimp surface therefore faces down: each edge is a lip at the top of a cassette, protruding forward, and the fingers come over its front radius and curl back underneath it, pads up. It is a hangboard edge turned upside down. (An earlier concept render on this page showed the ledge facing up. That was wrong and has been replaced.) Figure 3 shows the design as modelled.

The load path is:

  1. The finger pads push up on the lip underside. The lip is a short cantilever that bends in the layer plane, because the cassette prints on its side with the profile in XY.
  2. The cassette carries the load into a horizontal 60° dovetail tail just behind the lip, which bears up on the spine's slot flank. The dovetail runs along the edge width, so cassettes slide on sideways. A vertical dovetail would simply slide under load and leave the steel pin carrying 150 kg through plastic bearing.
  3. The spine carries the load up, in compression, to the top fender washer. It prints standing on end, so this too lies in the layer plane.
  4. The washer and nut pass it into an M10 threaded rod in tension, down to a DIN 582 eye nut. A DIN 580 eye bolt has too little thread to pass through the 75 mm spine.
  5. The ball-lock pin passes through both cassettes and the spine, outside the 75 mm finger zone. It only stops sideways sliding and is not in the load path.

Each lip's thickness comes from the bending check rather than by eye. For a lip of depth d, loaded at its front over a four-finger width b = 75 mm, the minimum root thickness is t = √(6Fd SF / bσXY), plus any incut drop, rounded up to 0.5 mm with an 8 mm floor. A cassette uses the thicker of its two lips, and every cassette leaves at least 46 mm of finger room between its lips. Table 5 gives the margins. All seven printed parts pass the geometry checks (valid, watertight, overhang, minimum wall and bed fit) with no warnings. The 6/8 and 10/12 cassettes carry no stipple, because after the 3 mm root fillet and the front radius they have no flat band left to texture.

Adjustable pinch block

A pinch lift is held by friction: the thumb and fingers squeeze the jaws, and the squeeze is what lifts the load. For a jaw face tapered at angle α and a skin friction μ, each jaw must be squeezed with N = W / 2(μ cos α − sin α). We take a deliberately low μ = 0.5, because less friction means a harder squeeze and more force in every part. At the 5° taper this gives N = 1.22W, about 1.8 kN per jaw at the rated load. Figure 4 shows the design.

The load path is:

  1. Friction lifts each jaw. A freely hanging, symmetric block splits the load evenly, W/2 per jaw.
  2. Each jaw bears on two plain steel rods that run across the block at the height of the squeeze, at its front and back edges, outside the 75 mm finger zone.
  3. The rods carry the load in bending into a central hub. The hub prints on its side, so it works as a beam within the layers, and it passes the load to the same steel stack as the edge block: an M10 rod in tension and a DIN 582 eye nut.
  4. The squeeze runs from one jaw through clip-on spacer sleeves into the hub, and out through the other side's sleeves into the other jaw. Every part on this path is plastic in compression, and the two squeezes balance each other.

Three changes from the original specification came out of the checks. First, the specification's ∅8 mm rods reach a safety factor of only about 0.5, because the squeeze can land up to 15 mm above or below the rod line and adds a couple to the vertical load. The check picks ∅16 mm from the stock sizes. Second, the planned ball-lock pin has nowhere safe to go: through an ∅8 mm rod it leaves almost no steel, and in a printed hole row it tears out at a safety factor of 1.5. Spacer sleeves replace it. They snap onto the rods and come in a binary set of 2.5, 5, 10 and 20 mm per rod end, so every width from 50 to 120 mm in 5 mm steps is symmetric. Spare sleeves park on the rod ends, outside the finger zone. Third, a central hub between two jaws cannot close below about 50 mm, so the narrowest width is 50 mm rather than 30. Table 6 gives the margins; all six printed parts pass the geometry checks with no warnings.

Load-cell gauge housing

The gauge clips in line between a block and the loading pin, or between a block and a floor anchor for isometric pulls. Its load path is steel only: a DIN 582 M12 eye nut on an M12 stud, the S-beam load cell, and a second stud and eye nut below. Each stud is locked into the cell with a jam nut. Eye nuts on studs replace the specification's DIN 580 eye bolts, because an M12 eye bolt has 20.5 mm of thread and a typical cell is tapped only about 18 mm deep, so its collar might never seat. The cell is rated 200 kg with a 300 % ultimate overload, so it holds 600 kg against the 450 kg the safety factor asks for. The studs reach a safety factor of 17.8, and each eye nut carries 150 kg on a 340 kg working load limit.

The printed housing carries no load, and it must not take any. If it gripped both ends of the cell, stiff plastic would sit in parallel with the flexure, carry part of the load and falsify the reading. So the housing touches the cell only around its top end block, in a 0.3 mm locating pocket, and hangs on the top jam nut. Everywhere else, the flexure included, it keeps 1.5 mm of clearance, and the gap below the bottom jam nut is larger than the one above the top, so both ends can never touch at once. An automated test grows the cell by 1 mm and checks that nothing below the top block meets the housing.

Two shells split on the cell's mid-plane and print flat on their outer faces. A sealed sled holds a Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3, which carries the USB-C port and LiPo charger on board, an HX711 amplifier, a 1000 mAh LiPo, a button and an LED. It slides into a bay beside the cell from below and closes on a TPU gasket (Figure 5). The cell dimensions are nominal datasheet values until a real cell is measured; the housing rebuilds from one table when it is.

Status and future work

The software scaffold, continuous integration, core feature library, the six coupons and the automated checks (overhang map, 2.4 mm minimum wall, bed fit and analytical structural cases) are complete. Generic clearances are set so that design work can proceed before the coupons are printed. All three priority-one parts are designed: the edge block, the pinch block and the gauge housing. The next steps, in order, are:

  1. print the coupons and replace the generic clearances with measured ones;
  2. measure a real load cell and rebuild the gauge housing to it, then model the P2 and P3 parts;
  3. generate a one-page print card for each part, with orientation, material, slicer settings, hardware, proof-test load and retirement rule;
  4. write gauge firmware and a Web Bluetooth app.

Before anything is sold, each unit will get a serial number and a proof-test log, file buyers will get a licence and safety disclaimer, and the gauge will be reviewed for FCC/CE and UN38.3 compliance.

Remark. The 150 kg figure is a design target, not a tested rating. No trainer described here has been printed or proof-tested, and the material values in Table 2 are unverified.
