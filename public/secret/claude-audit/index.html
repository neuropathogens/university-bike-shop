Sixty-One Nights:
Stories from an Unattended Audit Log

Paul Wang

Palo Alto, California

November 7, 2026

Abstract

Since July 27, 2026, a scheduled job on one MacBook has woken at 11:59 PM, started a headless Claude Code session, and asked it to write down what happened on the machine that day. Over the 103 days to November 6 it produced 61 reports totalling about 11,800 words. Fifteen of those reports are a single sentence long, and 42 nights have no report at all. This paper retells one story from each of the fifteen weeks. Some stories are about the projects the log caught being built: a website in eleven commits, a gold-price trading bot that turned $1,000 into $59 in backtest, and four bicycle projects in four days. Others are about the auditor itself. It labelled reports with the wrong day, counted applications inconsistently, reported its own start-up as user activity, and once could not open the Desktop folder at all. We close with what the log is good at, what it missed, and four small changes that would make it more accurate.

Keywords: launchd, headless agents, activity logging, self-observation, off-by-one errors, shell history, small projects

Introduction

The instrument is a macOS LaunchAgent that runs a shell script every night at 11:59 PM. The script calls claude -p with a one-dollar budget and permission to use only the shell and the file writer. It tells the model to gather four kinds of signal (shell history, git commits from the last 24 hours, files modified in the last 24 hours, and a comparison of /Applications with the previous night's list) and to write the result to ~/Desktop/claude - audit/YYYY/MM/YYYY-MM-DD.md. When nothing notable happened, the model is told to say so in one line.

No one reads these reports as they arrive. They pile up on the Desktop. This paper is the first time all 61 have been read together. Figure 1 shows which nights produced a report, and Table 1 gives the totals by month. Coverage improved steadily: the auditor missed two nights in three in August and about one night in eight in October.

Fifteen weeks

Each subsection below covers one Monday-to-Sunday week. Dates are the ones written on the reports, which, as week 2 shows, are not always the day being described.

Week 1 (July 27 – August 2): Day zero

The first report describes the night the machine was set up. Homebrew arrived, followed by Python, Node, Go, Rust, Java, PostgreSQL and the GitHub CLI. The applications came in two waves. The first was ordinary (Docker, VS Code, Firefox, Notion, iTerm). The second, between 11:32 and 11:37 PM, was almost entirely AI tools: Cursor, Codex, Gemini and the Antigravity CLI, alongside the Claude and ChatGPT apps from earlier. Two local models were pulled through Ollama, Qwen 2.5 7B (4.7 GB) and Llama 3.2 3B (2.0 GB), and each got a README on the Desktop at 11:45.

At 11:59 PM, the same minute the auditor was scheduled to start, a README was saved for a project called Email Bot: a menu-bar app that uses claude -p to draft Gmail replies and is designed never to send them. So the first night's log ends with one headless Claude session describing another project built on headless Claude. The whole shell history at that point was sixteen lines long.

The second report, on July 28, made the auditor's first confident guess. A new app called Rec & Well was, it said, “likely the University of Michigan Recreational Sports app.” Nothing in the evidence pointed to Michigan.

Week 2 (August 3 – 9): A website in eleven commits, reported on the wrong day

The report dated August 4 says it was an “active dev day” on the University Bike Shop website and lists eleven commits, from wiring up the Available Bikes spreadsheet to embedding the Summer Camp registration form. The report dated August 3 says “No significant activity today.”

The commits were made on the evening of August 3, between 9:05 and 10:32 PM. The mismatch comes from how the reports are dated. The script stamps each report with the date at the moment it starts. When the Mac is asleep at 11:59 PM, launchd runs the missed job when the Mac next wakes. The “August 3” report was written at 12:37 AM on August 3 and described August 2. The “August 4” report was written at 10:18 AM on August 4 and described the previous evening. Table 2 shows that this happened nine times.

The same effect cost the log a whole evening. Five more commits landed on August 4 between 9:33 and 10:19 PM, including the Location & Hours tab, the Sell Your Bike tab and a one-command deploy script. The next report is dated August 8, and its 24-hour window had moved on by then. Those five commits appear in no report.

Week 3 (August 10 – 16): photo-collage.png.png

On August 12 a zip called Camp Day 1 was downloaded and extracted into 110 photos, IMG_7967 to IMG_8120. Next to them was a file the auditor reported without comment: photo-collage.png.png. Three days later, on August 15, the bike shop site got a commit titled “Add summer camp photos, standardize phone number formatting.” It came at the end of a morning of eight commits, four of them fixing the same icon. There was no report that night, so the log has the photos arriving but not their publication.

The same week, on August 14, the auditor noted that a Spotify installer had been downloaded but “not yet extracted/run.” Spotify never appears in any later application list.

Week 4 (August 17 – 23): The first security warning

On August 20 the auditor flagged a 17 MB disk image, WiFi_JoinNow_Wrapper(1).dmg, as an “Unrecognized app wrapper; worth confirming it was an intentional download before opening.” It was the auditor's first unprompted security advice. The “(1)” in the filename means it was the second copy downloaded. JoinNow is the name commonly used by network-onboarding tools that schools and universities use to connect a new laptop to Wi-Fi, and the date fits the start of a school year. The download happened at 8:36 AM, and this catch-up report was written at 8:39, so the auditor reviewed it within minutes of its arrival. GIMP appears in the application list for the first time in the same report.

Week 5 (August 24 – 30): The last word before the gap

The report for August 25 reads, in full, “No significant activity today.” It is the last report for fourteen nights. The original setup notes predicted gaps like this: launchd catches up a missed run after sleep, but a Mac that is fully shut down at 11:59 PM simply skips that night. The setup also turned out to have a quieter limit. However many nights the Mac sleeps through, launchd runs the job only once on waking, so a long sleep leaves one report rather than several.

Week 7 (September 7 – 13): The auditor learns to count

Each report is supposed to compare /Applications with the previous night's list. When the reports resumed on September 9, there was no previous list to compare with, so the auditor recorded a count: 22 entries. The next night it counted 26 apps (27 including the Utilities folder) and could not say which four were new, because the previous night had kept no names. On September 11 it correctly spotted one new app, 3uTools, an iPhone management utility. On September 12 the total was back to 22, with “no new or removed apps detected.” On September 14 it was 23, with a note that the difference was “worth a manual check against yesterday's actual app list (only a count was recorded, not names).”

That note fixed the problem. From September 14 on, every report that includes an application list gives the names, and the diffs become reliable. The September 14 list shows the extra app was Lunar Client, a Minecraft launcher, which the logs then mention almost every night for a month.

Week 8 (September 14 – 20): A session that wrote nothing

Codex creates a dated folder for every session, named after the first words of the prompt. On September 11 one of these folders produced an SVG for a school assignment on AI data centres. On September 17 the auditor found a new folder, ~/Documents/Codex/2026-09-17/is-w/, with an empty work/ folder, an empty outputs/ folder, and the comment “session started but produced no output files.” The prompt evidently began with two words, “is w…”, and the rest of that question is lost. The same report also noted that the shell history had stopped recording anything, the first sign of the problem in week 12.

Week 9 (September 21 – 27): Sales pause

The last commit to the bike shop site for a month was made on Sunday, September 21, at 3:28 PM: “Update phone number, add new team members, announce sales pause.” From then on the reports say “no commits in the last 24 hours” for the shop repository every night, and the phrase “no commits” appears 51 times across the log. The October 14 report quotes the commit as the repository's most recent, and the October 27 report still gives it as HEAD.1

The repository was not quite as idle as the log suggests. On October 21 a set of SEO changes was merged through a pull request on GitHub, and two more pull requests were merged on October 28. The auditor only looks at the copy of the repository on disk, so work done on GitHub and not yet pulled is invisible to it.

Week 10 (September 28 – October 4): The observer in the logs

Many reports mention npm activity from three packages: the memory, filesystem and sequential-thinking MCP servers. The auditor usually describes this correctly as “Claude Code launching its own MCP tool servers, not a manual install.” It never quite notices whose session that is. The September 12 report lists an npm log stamped 2026-09-13T06_59_01. That is 6:59 AM UTC, or 11:59 PM in California: the moment the auditor itself started. The September 14 report gets closer, observing that the log's “local mtime” was 11:59 PM, but still files it under the day's activity.

On September 23 it finally names itself, listing ~/.claude.json as changed by “this Claude Code session.” A week later, on September 30, it finds a different kind of trace: two hidden 9.4 MB PDF downloads named .Q6L2SF6YDW.com.anthropic.claudefordesktop.*, temporary files left by the Claude desktop app. Across the log, a large share of the “files modified in the last 24 hours” are Claude's own session files, caches and backups. Part of what the auditor is measuring is itself.

Week 11 (October 5 – 11): The art of saying nothing

This week had two one-line reports (October 5 and 11) and three routine ones, which makes it a good place to count. Fifteen of the 61 reports are the single sentence “No significant activity today.” The other 46 average about 250 words. The longest, at 429 words, is October 27, a day on which a video project was started and Blender was installed.

“Quiet” is relative. The October 6 report calls it “a quiet day” and then lists two screenshots and a new RAW photo from a Canon camera (GL5A0408.CR3). Screenshots alone are named 38 times across the log. The auditor's sense of “significant” seems to mean “a commit, an install or a new project,” and a day of ordinary computer use often falls below that line.

Week 12 (October 12 – 18): How many times was Homebrew installed?

The October 14 report says Homebrew was “installed via the official install script,” followed by gh auth login, a fresh install of Claude Code and a fresh clone of the bike shop repository. The same report notes that Claude Code had moved from version 2.1.299 the day before to 2.1.300.

The trouble is the evidence. ~/.zsh_history is stored without timestamps, so a line in it says that a command ran, not when. The Homebrew line had been in the file since July 27, when the history was sixteen lines long. Later reports are more careful. On October 27 the auditor writes that the dates “can't be confirmed from this file alone.” On November 5 it checks file timestamps instead and concludes that Homebrew “was actually installed on 2026-10-26.” Depending on which night you ask, Homebrew was installed on July 27, October 14 or October 26. There is a one-line fix, given in Section 3.

Week 13 (October 19 – 25): $1,000 → $59

On October 22, starting at 12:06 PM, a complete research project appeared in ~/kalshi-gold-bot. It was a bot for Kalshi's 15-minute gold-price markets, with a market client, fee model, probability model, Kelly position sizing, a backtester, raw XAU/USDT price data from Binance and an HTML dashboard. The auditor read the README and copied out its conclusion: there was no reliable trading edge, and following the model when it disagreed with the market's price would have turned $1,000 into $59.

A trading project whose main finding is “don't trade this” is still a good result, and the bot was left in paper-trading mode by default. The auditor also noted that the folder was not a git repository, so if the conclusion is ever doubted, there is no history to show how it was reached.

The same week, on October 20, the auditor hit its first technical wall. Listing the Desktop and Documents, which are synced to iCloud, hung, and it switched to Spotlight's mdfind to finish the job.

Week 14 (October 26 – November 1): Four bike projects in four days, and a blind night

This was the busiest week in the log, and every project in it was about bicycles.

  • October 26. university-bike-fits, a bike-fit video analysis system, was built from scaffold to web app in seven commits labelled phase 0 to phase 6: video ingest and lens calibration, pose estimation, pedal-stroke segmentation, a metric registry, reports, and a web app with PDF export. Cloudflare WARP was installed the same day, and a promotional reel was saved to the Desktop.
  • October 27. university-bikes-short, a short advertisement made in code, was started between 11:35 PM and midnight. Blender was installed. Downloads filled with character images and two clips of a high-school bike rack, along with OperaSetup.exe, a Windows installer that could not run on this Mac.
  • October 28. A full bike model was exported from Fusion in four formats (.f3d, .3mf, .smt, .stl), and work started on bike-reels, a set of rendered Instagram reels.
  • October 29. bike-fit-reel, a before-and-after video for the fitting service, was created and rendered between 10:35 and 10:55 AM: 390 composited frames, a music track and a preview MP4. Its README explains that it reuses the animation rigs from the October 27 project and the fit targets from the October 26 one. The three projects form a chain.

Then, on October 30, the auditor went blind. Every attempt to list the Desktop, Documents or Downloads “hung indefinitely rather than returning.” Its own explanation was a macOS privacy prompt that “nothing in this non-interactive session can answer.” The report states plainly that the contents of those folders “are therefore unknown,” which is the right thing to write. Access worked again the next night. The week ended with two one-line reports.

Week 15 (November 2 – 7): Repositories older than themselves

On November 5 at 6:00 PM a new SSH key pair was generated with the comment oci-minecraft, which suggests a Minecraft server on Oracle Cloud and fits a month of Lunar Client entries. On November 6 two new repositories appeared: bikeparts, a library of 3D-printable bike parts, with ten commits, and grip-lab, a kit of 3D-printed climbing trainers, with two.

The timestamps in that report do not add up. The auditor says bikeparts was “created ~21:47” but lists commits from 21:37. It says grip-lab was “created ~21:49” and lists both of its commits at 18:58, about three hours before the repository existed. The likely cause is that the auditor read a folder's modification time as its creation time. A folder's modification time changes every time a file is added or removed, so in a busy repository it mostly shows when the last file changed.

The log has no entry for today, November 7, yet. Tonight's report will probably record that the bike shop repository received a commit adding a paper about its own audit log.

Discussion

A nightly report written by a model, with no one checking it, is a surprisingly good diary. It caught every new project, explained what most of them were by reading their READMEs, and was honest about its blind spots. Its errors are of a few specific kinds, and each has a simple fix:

  1. Off-by-one dates. Stamp each report with the day it describes, not the day it starts. If the job starts before 6:00 AM, it should use yesterday's date. A catch-up run should cover the time since the last report, not a fixed 24 hours, so that evenings like August 4 are not lost.
  2. Untimestamped history. Adding setopt EXTENDED_HISTORY to ~/.zshrc stores a timestamp with every command, which would have settled the Homebrew question in one line.
  3. Self-observation. Tell the auditor to ignore its own session files and the npm logs written in the minute it starts.
  4. Permissions. Give the scheduled job Full Disk Access once, in System Settings, so a privacy prompt can never blind it again as it did on October 30.

Two limits cannot be fixed inside the script. The log only sees the machine, so work done on GitHub, on a phone or on paper is missing. And a Mac that is shut down at midnight leaves no trace, which is how the log came to have a blank week.

Remark. On most days the raw reports also list schoolwork, college-application documents, personal photos and files with other people's names in them. That material is left out of this paper on purpose. Every story above is drawn from the reports; quotations are verbatim, and commit times were checked against the repository's history.
